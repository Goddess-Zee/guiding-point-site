# You Need a Digital Strategist, Not a Full-Time Hire

Most nonprofits and small government contractors hit the same wall eventually. The website needs a relaunch. Nobody quite remembers why half the tech stack looks the way it does.

Someone added a new tool to fix a problem two years ago. Then someone else added another. Nobody planned it that way — it just happened, tool by tool, whenever something broke.

Nobody owns the digital presence. Nobody owns the strategic planning behind it either. Not because leadership doesn't care. They just don't have the time.

A full-time Chief Digital Officer would solve this. But that role is nowhere near most budgets.

That's the gap fractional digital strategy fills.

## What "fractional" actually means

A fractional digital strategist isn't a consultant who shows up once, hands you a slide deck, and disappears. It's ongoing, senior-level strategic input.

Picture a few hours a week or a few hours a month, depending on what your organization needs. The strategist gets embedded enough to actually understand how your organization runs. But you're not paying for a full-time salary, benefits, or a drawn-out hiring process.

Think of it as the digital equivalent of a fractional CFO. Many small businesses already use fractional CFOs to get finance expertise without a full-time hire. A fractional digital strategist works the same way, but for your digital marketing strategy, your tools, and your online presence. They set direction. They make the calls that require expertise your team doesn't have in-house. They stay accountable to outcomes — without occupying a full-time seat on your org chart.

Fractional work has become common in finance and operations roles for small businesses over the past several years. Digital strategy is simply catching up to that model.

## Who actually needs this

Not every organization needs a fractional digital strategist. But a clear pattern shows up again and again in the organizations that do.

**You've outgrown "whoever's free handles it."** Your team makes digital decisions reactively. Website changes, CRM choices, email systems, new AI tools — whoever has bandwidth that week makes the call. Nobody ties those decisions to a unifying digital strategy.

**You need outside expertise for a defined stretch, not forever.** Maybe you're planning a website relaunch. Maybe you're migrating systems. Maybe you're preparing digital capability statements for a bid. None of that requires a permanent hire. It requires focused expertise for a set period of time.

**You have budget for guidance, not for a full department.** Fractional work scales to what you can actually spend. You're not stuck choosing between "hire someone full-time" and "do nothing."

**Nobody on staff owns the roadmap.** Tools and platforms pile up over time. Nobody asks whether they still work well together. Nobody checks whether a shiny new tool actually solves the right problem before your organization buys it.

If none of this sounds familiar, you probably don't need this yet. If two or more of these do sound familiar, it's worth a conversation.

## What it looks like in practice

Fractional digital strategy usually starts with an audit. The strategist reviews your digital presence — your website, your tools, your workflows — and looks at how digital work actually supports your mission or your contract pipeline.

From there, the work becomes ongoing. The strategist ranks what needs fixing first. They vet new AI tools before your organization spends money on them. They make sure a decision made this quarter doesn't create rework next year.

For nonprofits, digital strategy for nonprofits often centers on aligning the website and digital presence with grant strategy and donor experience. Funders and donors both form impressions fast, and an outdated site undercuts trust before a single conversation happens.

For government contractors, the focus shifts. Fractional strategists usually work on strengthening the digital capability story that shows up in proposals and past performance narratives. They also often improve the systems that support subcontractor coordination behind the scenes — systems that rarely get attention until something breaks during a live bid.

## The real value isn't the hours — it's the accountability

Organizations that bring in fractional support don't usually point to one specific fix as the biggest win. They point to something less tangible: someone now owns the strategic planning question. It stopped being everyone's fifth priority and no one's first.

That accountability is exactly what a full-time chief digital officer would give you. A fractional digital strategist delivers the same accountability, without requiring you to fund a full-time role to get it.
