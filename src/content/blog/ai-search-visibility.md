For two decades, people researched a cause, a vendor, or an acronym they heard on the news by turning to a search engine and clicking through to a website. That journey is changing fast, and it's changing for both of the audiences Guiding Point serves.

## Donors are already researching through AI, not search

Donors increasingly ask an AI assistant about a cause instead of typing it into a search bar. [Global Web Index's July 2026 survey](https://candid.org/blogs/ai-and-fundraising-donors-search/) found that nearly six million U.S. donors now cite AI as their preferred tool for discovering and researching causes.

The traffic data backs this up. Blue State's November 2025 report, covered by [Candid](https://candid.org/blogs/ai-and-fundraising-donors-search/), showed nonprofit website traffic from organic search had already dropped 35 percent in a single year, while AI-referred traffic spiked more than 1,000 percent over the same period. And according to Similarweb data [cited by SparkToro](https://sparktoro.com/blog/in-2026-less-than-one-third-of-google-searches-still-send-a-click/), 68 percent of web searches now end without a single click through to any website at all.

For a nonprofit relying on donors finding a donation page or newsletter signup the old way, that's a real problem. The journey hasn't ended. It's just moved somewhere your website might not be visible.

## Contracting officers are doing the same thing

This shift isn't limited to donors. Federal procurement is moving the same direction. According to [Presenc AI's research on government and public sector AI visibility](https://presenc.ai/geo-for/government-public-sector), 47 percent of procurement officers now report using AI assistants during market research and RFI preparation as of 2026.

Agencies are building this into their process directly. DHS's Procurement Innovation Lab developed an [AI-powered market research tool](https://www.potomacofficersclub.com/dhs-develops-ai-powered-market-research-tool-for-contracting-officers/) that scrapes SAM.gov and other public databases to surface the most relevant vendors for contracting officers, ranking them before a human ever reviews the list by hand.

If your organization isn't structured to be legible to these systems, you risk sitting outside the consideration set before a contracting officer or a program manager ever sees your name.

## What actually makes an organization visible to AI

The fix isn't a single setting to flip. It's a handful of structural changes that affect how AI systems read and cite your organization.

**Authority gets built across the web, not just on your site.** AI systems pull from news coverage, third-party listings, and public mentions, not only from your own homepage. Press coverage, directory listings, and community mentions function as inputs into your visibility now, not just as separate PR activities.

**Your website needs to be readable by machines, not just humans.** Pages built on heavy scripting, image-only content, or unstructured prose are hard for AI systems to crawl accurately. Clear headings, structured data, and direct question-and-answer formatting on your program or capability pages make it far easier for an AI system to represent your work correctly when someone asks about it.

**The old metrics don't tell the whole story anymore.** Homepage sessions and search rankings were built for a web where people clicked through to your site. In a world where a growing share of research ends without a click, visibility also means tracking how often your organization gets cited inside AI-generated answers, not just how many people land on your page.

## Where this fits for nonprofits and government contractors

For nonprofits, this means your digital presence needs to support how funders and donors are actually researching causes today, not just how they did five years ago. For government contractors, especially subcontractors competing for attention inside a shortlisting process you can't see directly, this is becoming a procurement-relevant channel in its own right.

The organizations that adapt their content and digital structure now will be the ones AI systems actually find, cite, and recommend. The ones that don't will keep optimizing for a version of search that's already shrinking.
